# Holistic Review

Judge the whole experience when the question goes beyond ease of use. Such questions ask whether the product
is worth using, whether people trust it, and which qualities matter most for these users. Usability is necessary but not
sufficient for a good experience (Morville).

## Steps

1. **State the inputs:** the users, their tasks, and the context, as in a heuristic audit.
2. **Weigh the usability qualities.** Quesenbery splits usability into five qualities: effective, efficient,
   engaging, error tolerant, and easy to learn. Rank them for this product, because the ranking changes the
   verdict. A form used once a year needs ease of learning more than speed; a tool used all day needs the
   reverse.
3. **Check the wider facets.** Morville's honeycomb names seven facets of user experience: useful, usable,
   desirable, findable, accessible, credible, and valuable. For each one that matters here, say what supports
   or undermines it. Usable is the heuristic audit; accessible goes to a separate accessibility review.
4. **Find the weakest link.** Name the one or two facets or qualities that most limit the experience. Give the
   evidence from the interface for each.
5. **Report** in the shape `SKILL.md` gives. Each finding names the quality or facet it affects.

The review is done when every quality is ranked, every relevant facet has a judgement with evidence, and the
weakest links are named.

## Limits

Useful and valuable are product and business questions. Judge them only from what the interface shows, and
name the evidence a product owner would need. Engagement and desirability depend on people's reactions, so a
review can only point at likely causes. Observing users settles them. SUS measures only perceived usability, so it
gives a global score and no causes.
