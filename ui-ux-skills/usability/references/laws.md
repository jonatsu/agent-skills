# Laws and Findings

Quantitative laws support a finding only within the conditions they were measured under. Each entry gives what
the law says, how to apply it, and the common misreading.

## Fitts's Law

The time to point at a target rises with the logarithm of its distance divided by its width (Fitts, 1954;
MacKenzie, 1992). Apply it to pointing and touch. Make frequent or urgent targets large and near, and keep
destructive targets away from common ones.

It predicts pointing time, not whether a control is found or understood. The claim that screen edges are
infinitely large targets holds only where the pointer stops at the edge, so it fails on touchscreens.

## Hick-Hyman Law

Choice reaction time grows with the logarithm of the number of equally likely alternatives, for a practised
task (Hick, 1952; Hyman, 1953). Apply it to practised choices, such as a familiar set of mode buttons.

It does not say that fewer menu items are always faster. Finding an item in an unfamiliar or unordered list is
visual search, which the law does not model.

## Working Memory: Miller and Cowan

Miller (1956) reported limits near seven for one-dimensional judgement and for immediate memory span. He called
the match between the two "a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence". Cowan (2001) put short-term capacity at about
four chunks once rehearsal and grouping are controlled.

Apply these limits to what users must hold in mind. Carry information to where it is needed. Show a code on the
screen that needs it, rather than asking users to remember it from the screen before. The limit is not a rule
that a menu or page holds seven items, because a visible menu needs recognition, not memory.

## Response-Time Limits

Nielsen restates three limits from earlier work (Miller, 1968; Card, Robertson, and Mackinlay, 1991):

- About 0.1 s feels instantaneous; the result itself is the feedback.
- About 1 s keeps the user's flow of thought; show that work is in progress beyond it.
- About 10 s is the limit of attention; beyond it, show progress and offer a way to cancel.

Each limit is approximate and depends on the kind of task. Treat all three as orders of magnitude, not thresholds.

## Jakob's Law

Users spend most of their time with other products, so they expect yours to work like those (Nielsen). It is a
practitioner's rule, not a measured law. Use it to argue for platform and category conventions.
